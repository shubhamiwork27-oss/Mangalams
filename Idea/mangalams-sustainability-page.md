# Sustainability at Mangalams — Built for the Indian Market

*Traditional Indian fashion & gifts, built around circularity — not consumption.*

We didn't add "sustainable" as a label. Mangalams is built around the specific problems the Indian retail and e-commerce boom is creating right now — environmental and economic. Here's what they are, in Indian numbers, and what we're actually doing about each one.

---

## Problem 1: India's Own Textile Waste Crisis

This isn't a problem happening somewhere else. India generates roughly 70.7 million metric tonnes of textile waste every year — 58% of it post-consumer, meaning clothes people actually threw away rather than factory offcuts. Panipat has become the world's largest textile recycling hub, processing thousands of tonnes a day, which sounds like a success story until you see the scale it exists to handle. Mumbai alone generates an estimated 120-150 tonnes of textile waste daily, much of it landfilled or burned rather than recycled, and surveys show over 80% of urban consumers don't even know sustainable disposal options exist.

The root cause is the same one driving the global fashion-waste crisis (Chile's Atacama Desert, Ghana's Kantamanto market are the famous international examples) — overproduction, brands and retailers manufacturing far more than will ever be worn, on the assumption that unsold or discarded stock is simply someone else's problem.

### Our approach
- **Buy, Rent, or Swap** — every eligible item on Mangalams can be rented (especially wedding and festive wear, often worn once) or swapped through our peer-to-peer marketplace instead of bought new.
- **Take-back guarantee** — anything purchased on Mangalams can re-enter the loop through resale, swap, or recycling instead of adding to landfill.
- **Made-to-order and small-batch production** — we don't stockpile inventory we can't sell.
- **Natural fibers first** — cotton, khadi, silk, and jute over synthetic blends. Materials that were sustainable in Indian textile tradition long before "sustainability" was a marketing word.

## Problem 2: Plastic Is Everywhere in Indian E-Commerce — Even When You Can't See It

India generates an estimated 9.4 million tonnes of plastic waste a year, close to 26,000 tonnes a day, and e-commerce packaging is a direct contributor. Amazon India and Flipkart both publicly eliminated single-use plastic from their fulfillment centers a few years ago, which is real progress — but it only covers their own warehouses, not the wider marketplace of sellers shipping directly, and doesn't touch the polyester in the garments themselves, which is plastic in fabric form and part of why so much discarded clothing doesn't biodegrade.

### Our approach
- **Plastic-free packaging by default**, not an opt-in extra.
- **Reusable cloth wrapping** (furoshiki-style) offered at checkout instead of paper-and-tape packaging.
- **Material transparency** — every product is tagged by fabric type, so natural-fiber, biodegradable pieces are easy to filter and find.

## Problem 3: India's E-Waste Is Growing Faster Than It Can Be Handled

The Central Pollution Control Board recorded about 1.7 million tonnes of e-waste generated in India in FY 2023-24, up from just 0.7 million tonnes five years earlier — growth of over 25% a year. Broader industry estimates that include unregistered disposal put the real figure above 5 million tonnes annually. Around 80% of it is handled by the informal sector — small scrap dealers using crude methods like open burning or acid stripping to recover metals — which by one government estimate wastes ₹30,000-40,000 crore worth of recoverable material every year, while releasing toxic fumes into neighborhoods that have no other choice but to live with them.

This isn't our product category, and we won't pretend it is. But it's part of the same throwaway culture we're pushing back against, in the same country.

### Our approach
- **Impact Hub** — a directory connecting users to certified e-waste and recycling collection points near them. Not a marketplace, just a resource, because pointing people to real, formal-sector solutions is worth doing even outside our core products.

---

## Problem 4: "Instant" Commerce Is Training Us to Over-Buy

Quick commerce — 10-to-15-minute delivery of almost anything — has grown explosively in India, powered by dense networks of dark stores in residential neighborhoods. It's convenient, but it's also built to encourage impulse purchases and constant short-haul delivery trips, the opposite of considered, intentional buying.

### Our approach
- **Considered commerce, not instant commerce** — we're not optimizing for the fastest possible delivery. We're optimizing for the fewest things bought that end up wasted.
- **Consolidated delivery options** — encouraging fewer, fuller shipments over constant small orders.

---

## Problem 5: Local Retailers Are Being Out-Capitalized, Not Out-Competed

India's kirana stores aren't a fading format — they still account for 75-78% of the country's total consumer goods sales, over 90% of FMCG sales, roughly 10% of GDP, and employ around 8% of the workforce. But quick commerce is measurably cutting into that: nearly half of quick-commerce users report buying less from their local kirana store, and some have stopped going entirely.

The mechanism isn't better products or service. Quick-commerce platforms build their own centralized dark-store networks and own the entire inventory and delivery chain themselves — the local shop isn't a partner in that model, it's a competitor being outspent. It's the same centralization pattern behind the environmental problems above, just aimed at livelihoods instead of landfills.

### Our approach
- **Local sellers as the supply chain, not a legacy channel we route around** — Mangalams doesn't own centralized inventory. Local shops and artisans do, and we connect them to buyers instead of replacing them.
- **Local-first fulfillment** — orders route to the nearest partnered local seller, not a company-owned warehouse. Shorter delivery distances also means a smaller carbon footprint, so this is the same design choice as our environmental commitments, not a separate one.
- **Transparent, fair commission** — published openly, so sellers know exactly what they keep.
